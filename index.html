Steering wheel grips

Grips and faceplate fasteners for the team's 2026–27 car. I'm on the Suspension group's Steering Wheel sub-team.

33–34mmGrip OD with cover
ASAPrinted in house
Nov 3Final Design Review, 2026

RoleWhat I did

  • Designed round steering wheel grips in PTC Creo — about 33–34 mm OD including the cover, based on Cosworth-style wheels from the team's market research.
  • Grips are 3D printed in house from ASA (not PLA, because of UV and heat at competition). They slide over the carbon fiber baseplate edge, are bonded on both sides, and get an alcantara cover.
  • Built the team's fastener sizing sheet: bolt length, thread engagement and edge distance by load.
  • Faceplate hardware: 6-32 PCB standoff bolts, rubber sealing washers, and 8-32 enclosure screws into heat-set inserts.
  • Presented the grips and faceplate fasteners at the Suspension Final Design Review on Nov 3, 2026.

SpinLaunch scale model

A functional centrifugal launch mechanism — designed, 3D-printed and tested with a 6-person team, then presented to judges and visitors.

6Team members
4Months

~4cmProjectile

WhyMotivation & benefit

Traditional rockets rely on fuel combustion to reach the upper atmosphere — expensive and environmentally costly. SpinLaunch's centrifuge-based approach stores energy mechanically instead: it spins a payload up to release speed and then lets it go, removing the need for a chemical burn during that phase of launch.

Our model demonstrates that mechanism at a small, interactive scale, built for the UIUC Engineering Open House.

RoleWhat I did

  • Led the design of the main housing and structure, and integrated the subassemblies from the rest of the team into the final build.
  • Designed the holding-release mechanism that grips and lets go of the projectile.
  • Modeled the parts in Fusion 360 and fabricated them with 3D printing.
  • Presented at KSEA MRC and UIUC EOH — walked judges and attendees through the working principle, launch calculations and future applications, and answered their questions.

How it works

Spin up, let go, predict the landing.

Principle · top view Spin-up
ωtarget

  1. 01

    Rotation

    A DC motor drives a rotating arm with the projectile mounted at its end. As the arm spins up, the projectile builds centrifugal acceleration — hitting a specific trajectory means reaching a precalculated RPM, tuned through repeated test launches. The arm has to be rigid enough to survive the impact load at release and carry the holding-release mechanism at its tip without flexing.

  2. 02

    Holding-Release

    A claw fixed to a rod grips the projectile through spin-up. Once the arm reaches its target speed, a trigger beside the firing tube drops and collides with the rod, separating it from the claw and releasing the projectile. With no centripetal force acting on it anymore, Newton's first law takes over: it flies off along a straight tangent line until gravity turns it into projectile motion.

  1. 03

    Landing calculation

    Once released, the projectile's velocity and launch angle determine where it lands — standard projectile-motion physics. We used that relationship to predict landing range, then built in a standard deviation to account for real-world uncertainty: wind, the projectile's own characteristics, ambient temperature and parachute deployment timing.

Variable-stiffness wheel

A wheel that switches between soft and rigid depending on the terrain, on a small remote-control demo car. The plan: visitors drive it and switch the wheels themselves.

15–20cmDemo car
4WDSkid-steer
~30°Target climb
3Team members

How it worksSmall actuators tension Kevlar cord spokes so the segmented rim locks rigid; releasing them lets the rim and a TPU honeycomb core go soft.

RoleWhat I did

  • It was my idea. I formed and lead a 3-person team, and split the roles.
  • Did all the CAD for the wheel and the demo car.
  • Demo car: 15–20 cm, 4WD skid-steer with no steering mechanism, all four wheels variable-stiffness, 3D printed on school printers.
  • Targets: climb about 30° and cross bumps.

ContextPrior research

I like seeing something I built actually move and do something real. Variable-stiffness wheels already exist in research — for example KIMM's morphing wheel (Science Robotics, 2024). This project reproduces that idea; I didn't invent it. I want to rebuild it and show it to people, because it has uses from wheelchairs to rovers.
